Article 2.1 · At the Limits of Axioms

Hilbert's Program

Can mathematics establish the reliability of its own rules?

A precise rule can still be a bad rule. Part I followed the attempt to make mathematical reasoning explicit, from Cantor's infinite sets through paradox and the restrictions of type theory and ZFC. A formal theory now had a language, axioms, and rules of inference. The next question concerned what those rules might allow us to prove.

Let TTT be a formal theory. Writing

T⊢φT\vdash\varphiT⊢φ

means that φ\varphiφ has a finite proof from TTT. What would happen if both φ\varphiφ and its negation had such proofs?

Why consistency matters

In classical logic, a contradiction makes every sentence provable. Starting with φ\varphiφ, we can infer φ∨ψ\varphi\lor\psiφ∨ψ, where ψ\psiψ is any sentence we choose. Combining this with ¬φ\neg\varphi¬φ yields ψ\psiψ. This is the principle of explosion.

The difficulty goes beyond one incorrect conclusion. Once everything is provable, proof ceases to distinguish conclusions at all.

A theory TTT is consistent if there is no sentence φ\varphiφ for which both T⊢φT\vdash\varphiT⊢φ and T⊢¬φT\vdash\neg\varphiT⊢¬φ.

For ordinary classical arithmetic, which proves 0≠10\ne10=1, this is equivalent to requiring

T⊬0=1.T\nvdash 0=1.T⊬0=1.

We use Con⁡(T)\operatorname{Con}(T)Con(T) to discuss consistency in the metatheory. At this stage it is a name for a property of the theory, rather than a new symbol in its original language. The next essay will explain how a suitable consistency statement can itself be written in arithmetic.

David Hilbert wanted a mathematical argument that ruled out every possible proof of a contradiction. Checking many examples would never suffice. A contradiction might appear in a proof longer than any we had inspected.

Moving the problem into another theory

Hilbert's interest in consistency preceded the set-theoretic crisis. His Foundations of Geometry (1899) organized geometry around relations among objects whose particular nature could be left unspecified. A point could be interpreted as a pair of real numbers. A line could be represented by an equation

ax+by+c=0,(a,b)≠(0,0).ax+by+c=0,\qquad (a,b)\ne(0,0).ax+by+c=0,(a,b)=(0,0).

If the relevant geometric axioms hold under this interpretation, a geometric proof can be translated into reasoning about coordinates. A contradiction in geometry would then produce one in the mathematical theory supporting those coordinates.

Schematically,

Con⁡(the underlying theory of real numbers)⟹Con⁡(the interpreted geometry).\operatorname{Con}(\text{the underlying theory of real numbers}) \Longrightarrow \operatorname{Con}(\text{the interpreted geometry}).Con(the underlying theory of real numbers)⟹Con(the interpreted geometry).

This is a relative consistency proof. It transfers a question about one theory to a question about another.

Where does the transfer stop?

The method is powerful, yet the premise remains. Why trust the theory used for the coordinates? We might construct the real numbers in set theory, then ask about set theory's consistency. A stronger theory might guarantee that consistency, only to raise the same question again.

Hilbert's second problem in his 1900 list asked for a proof of the consistency of arithmetic. His later program sought a form of justification that would avoid endlessly moving the burden into a comparably demanding theory.

Making proofs into objects

A formal proof is a finite sequence

φ1,φ2,…,φn.\varphi_1,\varphi_2,\ldots,\varphi_n.φ1​,φ2​,…,φn​.

Each line is an axiom or follows from earlier lines by an allowed rule. Once the system is effectively specified, checking a supplied proof becomes a sequence of finite operations on symbols. If axioms are given by an effective enumeration, a proof certificate can also record the finite evidence that each cited axiom occurs in that enumeration.

This changes the object of study. Inside arithmetic we prove statements such as 2+2=42+2=42+2=4. In metamathematics, we ask whether a particular sequence is a proof, or whether any valid proof ends in 0=10=10=1.

The distinction is one of level. An object theory studies numbers or sets. A metatheory studies that theory's formulas and derivations. Proof theory developed from treating these derivations as mathematical objects in their own right.

Standing outside a theory, however, is not enough to justify it. If a metatheory MMM proves Con⁡(T)\operatorname{Con}(T)Con(T), we can still ask why we accept MMM.

A restrained metamathematics

Finitism

Hilbert looked for especially secure methods in finitary reasoning. A finite string of strokes, a particular numeral, and a completed formal proof can be inspected and manipulated. Addition and multiplication on given numerals can be carried out step by step.

The proposed justification would rely on reasoning about such finite objects, without presupposing the completed infinite totalities used in analysis and set theory.

This does not mean checking a fixed list of examples. A finitary argument may establish a general claim about arbitrary finite constructions. The challenge is to justify the general reasoning while keeping its methods within the intended limits.

The philosophical choice also remains visible. Hilbert treated a certain grasp of finite signs and operations as a secure starting point. Calling it elementary does not remove every possible question about why it should be trusted.

Ideal methods

Hilbert wanted to preserve classical mathematics, including its use of infinite sets and nonconstructive existence proofs. He described its extensions using ideal elements.

Familiar expansions offer an analogy. Adjoining iii gives a solution to x2+1=0x^2+1=0x2+1=0 and a more uniform algebraic setting. Projective geometry introduces points at infinity to reorganize incidence relations. An enlarged language can make a theory more systematic.

For Hilbert, ideal methods in classical mathematics should likewise remain available, provided their use could be justified through an appropriately restrained metamathematics. The program concerned consistency and, in important formulations, the security of conclusions with finitary content.

Brouwer's intuitionism would demand a different kind of justification for mathematical existence. We will return to that disagreement in Article 2.5.

Epsilon terms

The program needed techniques for handling actual proofs. One of the Hilbert school's central tools was the epsilon calculus.

An existence statement

∃x P(x)\exists x\,P(x)∃xP(x)

asserts that some object satisfies PPP. An epsilon term

εx P(x)\varepsilon x\,P(x)εxP(x)

can be read as selecting an object satisfying PPP, if there is one. If no such object exists, the term still denotes an object of the nonempty domain, without satisfying PPP.

The characteristic principle says that any available witness can justify the selected one:

P(t)⟶P(εx P(x)).P(t)\longrightarrow P(\varepsilon x\,P(x)).P(t)⟶P(εxP(x)).

In the usual classical setting, quantifiers can be expressed through these terms:

∃x P(x) ⟷ P(εx P(x)),∀x P(x) ⟷ P(εx ¬P(x)).\begin{aligned} \exists x\,P(x)&\ \longleftrightarrow\ P(\varepsilon x\,P(x)),\\ \forall x\,P(x)&\ \longleftrightarrow\ P(\varepsilon x\,\neg P(x)). \end{aligned}∃xP(x)∀xP(x)​ ⟷ P(εxP(x)), ⟷ P(εx¬P(x)).​

The second line uses a possible counterexample. If any counterexample exists, the epsilon term selects one. Requiring that selected object to satisfy PPP rules out a counterexample.

For example, put a=εx(x+0≠x)a=\varepsilon x(x+0\ne x)a=εx(x+0=x). The formula a+0=aa+0=aa+0=a says that even the selected possible counterexample obeys the identity.

An epsilon term supplies formal syntax, not an algorithm for discovering a witness. Its purpose is to make the role of quantified existence accessible to a study of proofs.

Substituting concrete values

In arithmetic, the epsilon substitution method tries to replace epsilon terms occurring in a finite proof with numerals, so that the relevant conditions become correct finite statements.

Consider

e=εx(x+1=3).e=\varepsilon x(x+1=3).e=εx(x+1=3).

A trial assignment e=0e=0e=0 fails the condition e+1=3e+1=3e+1=3. Replacing it with e=2e=2e=2 succeeds.

The serious problem appears when many terms depend on one another. Repairing one assignment can invalidate a condition previously satisfied. Nested terms and induction make the repair process increasingly difficult to control.

A consistency argument would need to prove that an appropriate repair procedure always terminates for the proofs under consideration. If a purported derivation of 0=10=10=1 could be reduced in this way to correct finite calculations, its final contradiction could not survive.

The example illustrates the strategy. It supplies no termination proof for full arithmetic. Establishing termination was precisely the difficult work.

Three questions about formal systems

Consistency and completeness

Consistency asks whether a theory gives contradictory answers. Syntactic completeness asks whether it decides every sentence:

for every sentence φ,T⊢φ or T⊢¬φ.\text{for every sentence }\varphi,\qquad T\vdash\varphi\ \text{or}\ T\vdash\neg\varphi.for every sentence φ,T⊢φ or T⊢¬φ.

These are separate requirements. A consistent theory can leave a sentence undecided.

There is also semantic completeness of a proof calculus. If a sentence follows from TTT in every model, must it have a formal proof?

T⊨φ⟹T⊢φ.T\models\varphi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T\vdash\varphi.T⊨φ⟹T⊢φ.

Gödel's completeness theorem for first-order logic, published in 1930, answers this question positively. A particular theory can nevertheless have models that disagree about a sentence, leaving neither that sentence nor its negation derivable. This is how logical completeness can coexist with the incompleteness discussed next.

Decision procedures

The Entscheidungsproblem, or decision problem, asked for a mechanical procedure that always terminates and determines whether an arbitrary first-order formula is logically valid.

Logical validity means truth in every structure of the appropriate language. It differs from truth in the intended natural numbers, and from provability in a particular theory.

Effective axiomatization connects these issues. If a consistent, effectively axiomatized theory is syntactically complete, we can enumerate its proofs until either a sentence or its negation appears. That gives a decision procedure for that theory. The general validity problem for first-order logic is a distinct question, taken up in Article 2.3.

Hilbert's central consistency program and these wider logical investigations shared an ambition: to make the powers of formal reasoning themselves mathematically intelligible.

The observer re-enters the system

Hilbert's optimism culminated in the famous words, Wir müssen wissen. Wir werden wissen. We must know. We shall know.

His program gave that confidence a technical form. Formalize mathematics, study its proofs as finite objects, then establish their reliability by especially secure methods.

One further possibility made the separation between theory and metatheory more delicate. A formula is a finite string. A proof is a finite sequence of strings. Natural numbers can encode finite sequences.

Could arithmetic therefore describe the formulas and proofs that the metamathematician had been observing from outside?

Gödel's work begins with that question.
