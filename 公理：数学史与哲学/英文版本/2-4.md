# Forcing and the Continuum

*How different models can satisfy the same axioms*

The continuum hypothesis asks whether the real numbers have the smallest possible uncountable cardinality:

$$
\mathrm{CH}:\qquad 2^{\aleph_0}=\aleph_1.
$$

Cantor had proved that $\aleph_0<2^{\aleph_0}$. The remaining question became the first of Hilbert's 1900 problems.

Gödel and Cohen eventually answered a different question about it. Assuming the consistency of ZFC, those axioms prove neither CH nor its negation.

This essay follows the mechanisms behind that result. The route is longer than a single diagonal argument: we need to understand what a model contains, how new sets can be added, and why the enlarged structure still satisfies the axioms.

## What counts as a real number?

A subset $A\subseteq\mathbb N$ determines its characteristic sequence:

$$
\chi_A(n)=
\begin{cases}
1,&n\in A,\\
0,&n\notin A.
\end{cases}
$$

Conversely, a binary sequence determines the set of positions where it takes the value 1. Thus the power set of $\mathbb N$ and the space $2^\omega$ of infinite binary sequences have the same cardinality.

### Connecting sequences to the real line

The map

$$
(a_0,a_1,\ldots)
\longmapsto
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2a_n}{3^{n+1}}
$$

embeds binary sequences into the Cantor set. Using only ternary digits 0 and 2 makes distinct binary sequences give distinct points.

In the other direction, enumerate the rationals as $q_0,q_1,\ldots$. A real number $x$ is uniquely determined by

$$
\{n:q_n<x\}\subseteq\mathbb N.
$$

The Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein theorem combines the two injections into an equality of cardinalities:

$$
|\mathbb R|=|2^\omega|=|\mathcal P(\mathbb N)|.
$$

Set theorists therefore often call an element of $2^\omega$, or a subset of $\omega$, a **real**. Adding a new real may mean adding a new binary sequence.

## Gödel's inner universe

### From the cumulative hierarchy to L

The universe of sets is commonly denoted by $V$. It is a proper class, not a set containing every set. Its cumulative hierarchy begins with

$$
V_0=\varnothing,\qquad
V_{\alpha+1}=\mathcal P(V_\alpha),
$$

and takes unions at limit stages:

$$
V_\lambda=\bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda}V_\alpha.
$$

Gödel retained the layered construction while restricting the successor operation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_0&=\varnothing,\\
L_{\alpha+1}&=\operatorname{Def}(L_\alpha),\\
L_\lambda&=\bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda}L_\alpha.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\operatorname{Def}(L_\alpha)$ consists of the subsets of $L_\alpha$ definable over the structure $(L_\alpha,\in)$ by first-order formulas with finitely many parameters from $L_\alpha$.

For a formula $\varphi$ and parameters $a_1,\ldots,a_k$, such a subset has the form

$$
\{x\in L_\alpha:
L_\alpha\models\varphi(x,a_1,\ldots,a_k)\}.
$$

The union of these stages over all ordinals is the **constructible universe** $L$.

We have $L\subseteq V$. The inclusion need not be strict: $V=L$ is itself compatible with the usual axioms, assuming they are consistent. Constructibility also differs from computability. A constructible set may first appear very far along the ordinal hierarchy.

### How much does one stage include?

At finite stages, $L_n=V_n$: a subset of a finite set can be specified by listing its elements with parameters.

At the first infinite stage, $L_\omega$ is countably infinite. Its full power set is uncountable, whereas there are only countably many formulas and finite parameter lists over $L_\omega$. Consequently,

$$
\operatorname{Def}(L_\omega)
\subsetneq
\mathcal P(L_\omega).
$$

This comparison concerns one stage. It does not prove that the completed class $L$ omits any particular set in every possible ambient universe.

### How an inner model satisfies power set

The class $L$ is transitive and contains all ordinals. Transitivity means that members of its sets are themselves in $L$. Gödel proved that it satisfies ZF.

How can power set hold if some externally available subsets might be absent?

For $x\in L$, distinguish

$$
\mathcal P^V(x)=\{y\in V:y\subseteq x\}
$$

from

$$
\mathcal P^L(x)=\{y\in L:y\subseteq x\}.
$$

The latter is the collection of subsets available inside $L$. Gödel's argument establishes that this collection is itself a set in $L$.

The power-set axiom, interpreted in $L$, quantifies over objects of $L$. It demands a set collecting exactly those internal subsets. It does not quantify over sets missing from the model.

Thus $\mathcal P^L(x)\subseteq\mathcal P^V(x)$, with equality or strict inclusion depending on the situation. If a nonconstructible real exists in $V$, it belongs to $\mathcal P^V(\omega)$ and is absent from $\mathcal P^L(\omega)$.

| Structure | Subsets of $\omega$ in its quantifier range | Its internal power set |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $V$ | All subsets present in the ambient universe | $\mathcal P^V(\omega)$ |
| $L$ | Constructible subsets | $\mathcal P^L(\omega)$ |

Both structures can satisfy the same first-order axiom. Their available objects differ.

### Choice and the continuum in L

Constructible sets admit a canonical well-order based on their stages of appearance, defining formulas, and parameters. Selecting least elements in this order supplies choice functions. Hence $L$ satisfies the axiom of choice.

A deeper result, the **condensation lemma**, controls how early constructible subsets appear. Inside $L$, every real appears at a stage indexed by a countable ordinal. Each such stage is countable there, and there are $\aleph_1^L$ countable ordinals. This bounds the number of constructible reals by $\aleph_1^L$.

Cantor's theorem gives the opposite uncountability bound, so

$$
L\models 2^{\aleph_0}=\aleph_1.
$$

The same deeper analysis yields the **generalized continuum hypothesis**:

$$
L\models 2^\kappa=\kappa^+
\quad\text{for every infinite cardinal }\kappa.
$$

Here $\kappa^+$ denotes the next larger cardinal. This is a statement about cardinal arithmetic as computed inside $L$.

Counting formulas alone would be insufficient: later stages have new parameters, and the construction runs through all ordinals. Condensation supplies the crucial control.

Gödel's result gives one relative-consistency direction:

$$
\operatorname{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})
\Longrightarrow
\operatorname{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}+\mathrm{CH}).
$$

The other direction requires an enlargement.

## Adding a real without losing the axioms

Paul Cohen's idea was to work with partial information about a new set and control what that information would ensure in an eventual extension.

For the construction below, suppose $M$ is a **countable transitive model of ZFC**. Countability is assessed from outside. This assumption is stronger than mere consistency of ZFC. It provides a clear picture of genericity, while the full relative-consistency proof needs a separate argument to avoid that stronger premise.

### Why an arbitrary diagonal real is not enough

Externally, we can enumerate the binary reals of $M$ as $x_0,x_1,\ldots$ and define

$$
r(n)=1-x_n(n).
$$

Then $r\notin M$. There is no contradiction with $M$ believing its reals uncountable: the external enumeration is not a function available in $M$.

But $M\cup\{r\}$ generally fails to satisfy ZFC. The axioms also require pairs, unions, function images, and further subsets involving $r$. An arbitrary new real gives no adequate control over that closure.

### Finite conditions

Use the partial order

$$
\mathbb P=\operatorname{Fn}(\omega,2,{<}\omega).
$$

A **condition** is a binary function defined at finitely many natural-number positions. For example,

$$
p=\{(0,1),(3,0),(7,1)\}
$$

requires the eventual real to have those three digits.

More information makes a condition stronger:

$$
q\leq p\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q\supseteq p.
$$

The order reverses containment. A stronger condition allows fewer possible complete sequences.

Two conditions are **compatible** when they assign no conflicting values. Their union is then a common strengthening. Conditions that prescribe different digits at the same position are incompatible. Incomparability alone does not imply incompatibility.

### Dense requirements

A set $D\subseteq\mathbb P$ is **dense** if every condition can be strengthened into it:

$$
\forall p\in\mathbb P\ 
\exists q\leq p\ (q\in D).
$$

It expresses a requirement that can still be met after any finite decisions already made.

For each $n$, the set

$$
D_n=\{p:n\in\operatorname{dom}(p)\}
$$

is dense. A condition can always decide the next required position.

For an old real $x\in M$, the set

$$
E_x=\{p:\exists n\in\operatorname{dom}(p),\ p(n)\ne x(n)\}
$$

is also dense. If no difference has appeared, choose an unused position and enter the opposite digit.

Meeting every $D_n$ ensures a complete sequence. Meeting every $E_x$ ensures that sequence differs from every real in $M$.

### A generic filter

A **filter** $G\subseteq\mathbb P$ combines coherent conditions. Any two conditions in $G$ have a common stronger condition in $G$, and every weaker condition above an accepted condition is also accepted.

With the stronger-is-smaller convention,

$$
\begin{aligned}
p,q\in G&\Longrightarrow
\exists r\in G\ (r\leq p,\ r\leq q),\\
p\in G,\ p\leq q&\Longrightarrow q\in G.
\end{aligned}
$$

The filter is **generic over $M$** if it meets every dense subset of $\mathbb P$ belonging to $M$.

Since $M$ is externally countable, its dense sets can be externally listed as $D_0,D_1,\ldots$. Successively choose

$$
p_0\geq p_1\geq p_2\geq\cdots,
\qquad p_{n+1}\in D_n.
$$

The filter generated by the chain meets every requirement on that list.

The union

$$
r=\bigcup G
$$

is a complete binary sequence different from every old real. Thus $r\notin M$. For this particular forcing, $G$ can be recovered as the finite conditions contained in $r$, so $M[G]$ is also written $M[r]$.

Genericity means meeting the old model's dense requirements. It gives no license to choose arbitrary truth values.

## Describing the extension before it exists

### Names

To speak about new objects before choosing $G$, use **names**. A name $\tau$ is built recursively from pairs $(\sigma,p)$, with $\sigma$ another name and $p$ a condition.

Its interpretation is

$$
\tau^G=
\{\sigma^G:
\exists p\in G\ ((\sigma,p)\in\tau)\}.
$$

The pair records that the interpreted object $\sigma^G$ becomes a member when $p$ is accepted.

Old sets have canonical names:

$$
\check x=\{(\check y,1_{\mathbb P}):y\in x\},
\qquad \check x^G=x.
$$

Here $1_{\mathbb P}$ is the weakest condition, the empty function in this example.

Identifying a binary real with its set of 1-positions, a name for the generic real is

$$
\dot r=
\{(\check n,p):
n\in\operatorname{dom}(p),\ p(n)=1\}.
$$

The extension consists of the interpretations of the names available in $M$:

$$
M[G]=
\{\tau^G:\tau\in M\text{ is a }\mathbb P\text{-name}\}.
$$

It contains $M$ and $G$. New objects come from an organized system of old names.

### The forcing relation

If $p(7)=1$, every appropriate generic extension containing $p$ puts 7 in the generic real. We write

$$
p\Vdash\check7\in\dot r.
$$

More generally, $p\Vdash\varphi$ means that $p$ ensures $\varphi$ in the corresponding generic extensions.

This is the semantic intuition. The formal forcing relation is defined recursively inside the ground model using names and formula complexity, so it does not presuppose an extension already constructed.

For conjunction, $p$ must force both conjuncts. For negation,

$$
p\Vdash\neg\varphi
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{no }q\leq p\text{ forces }\varphi.
$$

For an existential statement, witness names must be available densely below the condition. Membership and equality are handled by recursion through the names.

### The forcing theorem

The central connection is

$$
M[G]\models\varphi(\tau_1^G,\ldots,\tau_k^G)
$$

if and only if some $p\in G$ satisfies

$$
p\Vdash\varphi(\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_k).
$$

Conditions deciding a given sentence form a dense set. From a condition $p$, either an extension forces the sentence, or no extension does, in which case $p$ forces its negation.

The forcing theorem and the analysis of names allow one to verify the axioms in the extension. Power set and replacement require substantial control of the names involved. The generic model theorem then gives

$$
M[G]\models\mathrm{ZFC}.
$$

This is the essential achievement. Producing a new sequence and producing a controlled model of set theory are separate parts of the construction.

## Changing the size of the continuum

### One new real does not refute CH

Suppose $M$ satisfies CH. Adding one Cohen real also creates other reals definable from it, but the continuum need not increase in cardinality.

The forcing is countable. Suitable **nice names** for subsets of $\omega$ can be recorded by countable sequences of antichains, with the antichain at position $n$ witnessing membership of $n$.

There are at most $2^{\aleph_0}$ such sequences in the ground model:

$$
(2^{\aleph_0})^{\aleph_0}=2^{\aleph_0}.
$$

Under ground-model CH, this is $\aleph_1$. The forcing also preserves $\aleph_1$, so the extension still satisfies CH.

Adding members and increasing cardinality are different operations on an infinite collection.

### Many rows of new digits

Instead, take

$$
\mathbb P=
\operatorname{Fn}(\omega_2\times\omega,2,{<}\omega),
$$

where $\omega_2$ is computed in $M$.

A condition fills finitely many cells of a binary array with $\omega_2$ rows. The generic union supplies a full function

$$
g:\omega_2\times\omega\longrightarrow\{0,1\}.
$$

Each row defines a real $r_\alpha(n)=g(\alpha,n)$. Dense requirements fill every position. For distinct rows $\alpha,\beta$, another dense requirement makes them differ at some fresh column.

The extension therefore contains at least $\aleph_2$ distinct reals, provided the old cardinals retain their sizes.

### Why the cardinals survive

An **antichain** is a family of pairwise incompatible conditions. The **countable chain condition**, or **ccc**, says that every antichain is at most countable.

The array forcing is ccc. To see the main idea, start with uncountably many finite conditions. The delta-system lemma provides an uncountable subfamily whose domains have one common finite intersection, called the root.

Only finitely many binary assignments are possible on the root. Thin the family again so all conditions agree there. Their remaining domains are disjoint, making any two conditions compatible. The original family could not have been an antichain.

The ccc bounds the possible values of names. A maximal antichain deciding an ordinal-valued name is countable, so that name has only countably many possible decided values.

For example, a supposed new countable sequence cofinal in the old $\omega_1$ would have only countably many candidate values in total. In $M$, those countable ordinals have a common bound below $\omega_1$. The sequence cannot be cofinal.

The general preservation argument shows that ccc forcing preserves cardinals and cofinalities. In particular, the old $\aleph_1$ and $\aleph_2$ remain distinct, giving

$$
M[G]\models
2^{\aleph_0}\geq\aleph_2>\aleph_1.
$$

Starting with a suitable model of GCH, counting nice names also gives the upper bound, yielding exactly $2^{\aleph_0}=\aleph_2$.

## What independence establishes

Gödel and Cohen's results combine to give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})
&\Longrightarrow
\operatorname{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}+\mathrm{CH}),\\
\operatorname{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})
&\Longrightarrow
\operatorname{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}+\neg\mathrm{CH}).
\end{aligned}
$$

Consequently, consistent ZFC proves neither side.

In the transitive-model picture, the ground model and extension have the same natural numbers and ordinals. They have different collections of subsets of $\omega$. Their internal power sets reflect those different ranges of quantification.

### Returning to the consistency premise

The countable transitive model assumed above is a useful explanatory setting. Its existence does not follow merely from $\operatorname{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$.

Full relative-consistency proofs avoid silently importing that stronger assumption. Approaches include Boolean-valued models, arguments through suitable finite fragments, and formalized syntactic treatments of forcing. These establish the displayed implications from consistency itself.

The account here explains the construction's main mechanisms. It does not replace those full metamathematical arguments.

Gödel's work does not settle CH as true in every intended sense, and Cohen's work does not establish its negation outright. Their results show what the ZFC axioms leave undecided. Further axioms require further mathematical and philosophical justification.

Classical logic still proves $\mathrm{CH}\lor\neg\mathrm{CH}$. A theory's inability to prove either alternative does not remove that logical principle.
