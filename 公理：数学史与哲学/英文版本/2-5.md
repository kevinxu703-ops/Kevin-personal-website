The independence of the continuum hypothesis leaves three statements side by side:

$$
\mathrm{ZFC}\nvdash\mathrm{CH},\qquad
\mathrm{ZFC}\nvdash\neg\mathrm{CH},
$$

while classical logic gives

$$
\mathrm{ZFC}\vdash\mathrm{CH}\lor\neg\mathrm{CH}.
$$

There is no contradiction. The first two statements concern what those axioms can decide. The third uses a rule of classical logic, the **law of excluded middle**.

Cohen's forcing preserves that logic. Brouwer challenged the unrestricted use of the rule itself. His question concerned what it takes to have completed a proof.

## Mathematics before logic

L. E. J. Brouwer's 1907 dissertation and his 1908 essay on logical principles placed mathematical construction before formal inference.

He traced the beginnings of mathematics to the experience of succession: one moment passes, is retained, and is distinguished from another. Repeating such a construction gives the progression of natural numbers.

In this account, logic records patterns in mathematical activity. Formal rules cannot by themselves give mathematical meaning to objects for which no construction has been supplied.

Hilbert hoped to secure classical ideal methods through a restrained consistency argument. Brouwer demanded constructive meaning for the methods themselves. A proof that some formal rules do not contradict one another would not automatically supply that meaning.

This position became known as **intuitionism**. Its intuition is not a guess or a picture suggesting an answer. It concerns the constructive activity through which mathematical objects are understood.

Classical logic, meanwhile, need not commit every user to one philosophy of independently existing objects. The disagreement is partly about proof rules, and partly about how particular thinkers justified them.

## What excluded middle supplies

The classical principle is

$$
P\lor\neg P.
$$

It applies without requiring a method that decides which alternative holds.

### Decidable cases

For a given natural number, parity can be determined by finite calculation. Equality of natural numbers is also decidable. There are uniform procedures producing the appropriate answer for every input.

Membership in an explicitly given finite list is decidable when equality of its elements is decidable. The qualification matters: a finite collection of arbitrary real numbers does not automatically come with decidable equality.

For a single proposition, constructive decidability means having a proof of $P\lor\neg P$. For a predicate depending on an input, a uniform decision method must handle all permitted inputs. Many elementary mathematical predicates meet this stronger requirement.

### Searching an infinite range

Consider

$$
P\equiv\exists n\in\mathbb N\,R(n),
$$

where every particular $R(n)$ can be checked.

Searching until a successful instance appears provides a witness if one exists. Any finite series of failed checks leaves further candidates open. A proof of nonexistence requires an argument covering them all.

The halting problem makes the uniform difficulty precise. Let $H(e)$ assert that program $e$ halts. A computational method providing, for every $e$, either a proof of $H(e)$ or a proof of its negation, with the chosen side identified, would decide halting.

Classical excluded middle gives the disjunction without such a uniform method. In the constructive reading of disjunction, a proof must identify a side and provide its proof.

This comparison uses a computational interpretation of construction. Brouwer's entire philosophy, including free choice sequences, is broader than a simple identification of every mathematical object with a Turing program.

### Double negation

Intuitionistic logic proves

$$
P\longrightarrow\neg\neg P,
$$

but it does not accept unrestricted **double-negation elimination**:

$$
\neg\neg P\longrightarrow P.
$$

If assuming $P$ produces a contradiction, we have proved $\neg P$. This form of reasoning by contradiction remains available.

If assuming $\neg P$ produces a contradiction, the direct result is $\neg\neg P$. Obtaining $P$ from there is an additional classical step.

Intuitionistic logic can even prove

$$
\neg\neg(P\lor\neg P).
$$

Assume $\neg(P\lor\neg P)$. A proof of $P$ would immediately contradict that assumption, so we obtain $\neg P$. But that also supplies $P\lor\neg P$, producing the contradiction again.

Thus intuitionism does not assert that excluded middle is false. It withholds the unrestricted positive disjunction.

## Existence and its witness

A constructive proof of

$$
\exists x\,P(x)
$$

provides an object $a$ together with a proof of $P(a)$. The object might be specified by a formula, algorithm, finite structure, or suitable approximation process. It need not be a printed decimal expansion.

### An irrational power

Do there exist irrational numbers $a,b$ such that $a^b$ is rational?

Put $c=(\sqrt2)^{\sqrt2}$. If $c$ is rational, choose $a=b=\sqrt2$. If $c$ is irrational, choose $a=c$ and $b=\sqrt2$, giving

$$
a^b
=\left((\sqrt2)^{\sqrt2}\right)^{\sqrt2}
=2.
$$

Classically, one of the cases holds. This elementary argument establishes existence without determining which pair it has supplied.

The statement also has an explicit-witness proof. Take

$$
a=\sqrt2,\qquad b=\log_{\sqrt2}3.
$$

Then $a^b=3$. To show $b$ is irrational, suppose $b=m/n$ with positive integers $m,n$. Raising the equation $(\sqrt2)^{m/n}=3$ to the appropriate powers gives

$$
2^m=3^{2n},
$$

contradicting unique prime factorization.

Both numbers have been specified, and their irrationality established. The same existence statement can therefore admit proofs with different constructive content.

### Approximation and exact existence

The classical intermediate value theorem provides an exact zero for a continuous function with opposite signs at the ends of an interval.

Constructive analysis distinguishes that conclusion from an approximate one:

$$
\text{for every }\varepsilon>0,\quad
\exists x_\varepsilon\quad |f(x_\varepsilon)|<\varepsilon.
$$

For a uniformly continuous function on a closed bounded interval, supplied with suitable continuity data and strict opposite endpoint signs, approximate zeros can be found constructively.

Obtaining an exact zero may require additional information or a stronger principle. Points with increasingly small function values need not by themselves form a specified Cauchy sequence. The passage from approximate witnesses to one exact object must be justified.

## What the logical symbols ask for

Around 1930, Arend Heyting formalized intuitionistic inference. The later **Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation**, or **BHK interpretation**, explains its connectives through the information proofs provide.

| Proposition | What a proof provides |
| --- | --- |
| $P\land Q$ | A proof of $P$ and a proof of $Q$ |
| $P\lor Q$ | A selected side, together with its proof |
| $P\to Q$ | A method transforming a proof of $P$ into a proof of $Q$ |
| $\neg P$ | A method turning any proof of $P$ into a contradiction |
| $\exists x\,P(x)$ | A witness $a$ and a proof of $P(a)$ |
| $\forall x\,P(x)$ | A method providing a proof of $P(x)$ for an arbitrary given $x$ |

The interpretation is an account of proof, not a mechanical test that decides whether every proposed proof-method is correct.

Write contradiction as $\bot$. Negation is expressed by

$$
\neg P\equiv P\to\bot.
$$

A proof of negation therefore carries a transformation. It says more than that no proof of $P$ has yet been found.

Similarly, a proof of

$$
P\land Q\longrightarrow Q\land P
$$

can take a pair of proofs and exchange their order. A proof of $\exists n\in\mathbb N\,(n^2=49)$ can provide $7$ and the calculation $7^2=49$.

Adding unrestricted excluded middle to intuitionistic propositional logic recovers classical propositional logic. The difference in proof information is therefore concentrated in a principle with substantial consequences.

### Growing information

Kripke semantics gives a model of intuitionistic logic using ordered information states. Atomic judgments persist as information grows. Negation at a state requires that no later state establish the proposition.

Consider a root $w_0$ with two terminal extensions:

| State | Information available |
| --- | --- |
| $w_0$ | $P$ is not yet established |
| $w_1$, extending $w_0$ | $P$ is established |
| $w_2$, also extending $w_0$ | $P$ is not forced, and has no further opportunity to become forced |

In this simple model, $w_1$ forces $P$ and $w_2$ forces $\neg P$. At $w_0$, neither is forced: $P$ is absent there, while the extension $w_1$ prevents its negation.

Hence

$$
w_0\not\Vdash P\lor\neg P.
$$

This is a model-theoretic counterexample to unrestricted excluded middle. It need not be understood as assigning one permanent third truth value to an otherwise classical proposition.

The symbol $\Vdash$ resembles the forcing notation in Article 2.4. Both settings involve strengthened information, but set-theoretic forcing extensions and intuitionistic Kripke models have different definitions and purposes.

## A continuum under construction

Brouwer's intuitionism also concerned mathematical objects, especially real numbers.

Classical set theory treats a binary sequence as a complete function. Brouwer allowed **choice sequences** whose further terms may be supplied as mathematical activity continues. Some follow a previously specified law. Free choices need not be fixed by a single such law in advance.

### From choices to intervals

Start with $[0,1]$. At each stage, choose the left or right third of the current interval. The nested intervals have lengths $3^{-n}$.

The binary choices determine a Cantor-set real

$$
x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2a_n}{3^{n+1}}.
$$

A finite string of zeros does not establish that every later choice will be zero. If a 1 appears, it supplies a positive lower bound for $x$. Proving $x=0$ requires information about the whole sequence, such as a rule guaranteeing that all its digits vanish.

The issue is not that a long observed prefix suffices to prove nondecidability. It illustrates the information missing from that prefix. A known rule can provide more than finite observation.

### Finite information and continuity

Suppose a process takes an infinite binary sequence as input and returns a natural number. Any completed computation can inspect only finitely many digits. Inputs agreeing on those inspected digits give the same output for that computation.

This motivates continuity principles in Brouwerian mathematics. A classical set-theoretic function that outputs 0 on the all-zero sequence and 1 on every other sequence cannot, at the all-zero input, determine its answer from a finite prefix.

The finite-information argument explains continuity of an appropriate effective process. Extending continuity to all functions of a specified intuitionistic domain is a further Brouwerian principle. It does not follow from intuitionistic logic alone.

## Different constructive traditions

Three levels should remain distinct.

**Brouwer's intuitionism** is a mathematical philosophy involving time intuition, choice sequences, and specific principles about the continuum.

**Intuitionistic logic** is a formal system that can be studied without accepting all of Brouwer's philosophical commitments.

**Constructive mathematics** includes several traditions with different views on computability, choice, real numbers, and acceptable witnesses. Bishop-style constructive analysis, for example, should not simply be identified with every Brouwerian principle.

Classical and intuitionistic mathematics also remain connected. Gödel–Gentzen negative translation systematically transforms classical proofs into intuitionistic ones. The translated conclusions can preserve negative information while withholding the original statement's positive witness.

Constructive proofs often reveal computational content. That content need not be efficient, and the underlying formal systems still face questions of consistency, expressiveness, and undecidability.

The change in standards brings changes in theorems. Trichotomy for arbitrary real numbers, unrestricted existence claims, and some choice principles may require reformulation or extra assumptions.

## Proofs as structured information

Disjunction now carries a choice. Existence carries a witness. Implication carries a transformation.

These demands make proof resemble structured data and computation. They also suggest another way to study mathematical objects: through the information they carry, the maps between them, and what those maps preserve.
