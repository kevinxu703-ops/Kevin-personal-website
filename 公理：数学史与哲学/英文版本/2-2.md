# Gödel and the Limits of Proof

*How arithmetic learns to describe its own proofs*

Hilbert's program treated formulas and proofs as finite objects to be studied from outside a formal theory. Gödel noticed that arithmetic could encode those objects. A statement about a proof could become a statement about a natural number.

This **arithmetization** of syntax was the technical starting point of his 1931 incompleteness paper.

Throughout this discussion, $T$ is a classical, effectively axiomatized theory with enough arithmetic to represent the relevant finite computations. Peano arithmetic, or **PA**, and its effective extensions provide familiar examples.

## Giving a formula a number

Assign the codes $1,2,3$ to the symbols $0,=,+$. Then $0=0$ becomes the sequence $(1,2,1)$. Unique prime factorization gives a way to encode a finite sequence of positive integers:

$$
(a_1,\ldots,a_n)
\longmapsto
2^{a_1}3^{a_2}\cdots p_n^{a_n}.
$$

Here $p_n$ is the $n$th prime. Our example becomes

$$
2^1\cdot3^2\cdot5^1=90.
$$

Factoring $90$ recovers the exponents, hence the sequence and formula. Positive codes and a fixed decoding convention keep the sequence length recoverable. This illustrates the principle, rather than reproducing Gödel's exact numbering.

A proof is a finite sequence of formulas. After coding each formula, we can encode that sequence too. One natural number can preserve an entire derivation.

Numbers acquire no intrinsic meaning as proofs. The chosen coding supplies a precise, reversible correspondence.

## Recognizing a proof arithmetically

Write $\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner$ for the code of a formula. Consider the relation

$$
\operatorname{Proof}_T(p,q),
$$

meaning that $p$ codes a valid $T$-proof whose final formula has code $q$.

Proof checking is mechanical: decode the sequence, inspect each inference, and check the last line. For a computably enumerable axiom system, a certificate can include finite evidence that the cited axioms occur in its enumeration. These checks can be represented in arithmetic.

We then define

$$
\operatorname{Prov}_T(q)
\equiv
\exists p\,\operatorname{Proof}_T(p,q).
$$

In a formal formula, a code appears through its **numeral**, the expression naming that natural number. We write $\operatorname{Prov}_T(\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner)$ to abbreviate this substitution.

The external claim that $\varphi$ has a proof now has an arithmetical counterpart. Checking a supplied proof remains a finite calculation. Determining whether any proof exists is a different task.

## Constructing self-reference

### Substitution

Let $\operatorname{Sub}(a,b)$ be the code obtained by taking the one-variable formula coded by $a$ and substituting the numeral for $b$ into its free variable. If $a$ codes $x+1=3$, then $\operatorname{Sub}(a,2)$ codes $2+1=3$.

Define

$$
d(x)=\operatorname{Sub}(x,x).
$$

It substitutes a formula's own code into that formula. This computation can be represented arithmetically. The notation $d(x)$ below abbreviates that representation, without requiring a new primitive function symbol.

### The diagonal lemma

Suppose $\theta(y)$ expresses a property of formula codes. We seek a sentence $G$ satisfying

$$
T\vdash G\leftrightarrow\theta(\ulcorner G\urcorner).
$$

The construction has three steps.

1. Form $\beta(x)\equiv\theta(d(x))$.
2. Let $b=\ulcorner\beta(x)\urcorner$.
3. Substitute the numeral for $b$ into $\beta$ to obtain $G=\beta(\bar b)$.

By construction,

$$
d(b)=\ulcorner\beta(\bar b)\urcorner=\ulcorner G\urcorner.
$$

Representing this calculation inside arithmetic yields the desired equivalence. This is the **diagonal lemma**.

Self-reference has been implemented through coding and substitution. No unexplained natural-language phrase such as “this very sentence” has entered the formal language.

## A sentence about its own unprovability

Choose

$$
\theta(y)\equiv\neg\operatorname{Prov}_T(y).
$$

The diagonal lemma supplies a sentence satisfying

$$
T\vdash
G\leftrightarrow
\neg\operatorname{Prov}_T(\ulcorner G\urcorner).
$$

Its informal reading is: **this sentence has no proof in $T$.**

The resemblance to the liar sentence has a limit. The liar concerns its own falsity. Gödel's sentence concerns provability in a specified theory. Since truth and provability have not been identified, no immediate contradiction follows.

### Why a consistent theory cannot prove it

Suppose $T\vdash G$. An actual finite proof of $G$ would have some standard natural-number code $n$. Arithmetic can verify that particular proof, giving

$$
T\vdash\operatorname{Prov}_T(\ulcorner G\urcorner).
$$

From $G$ and its fixed-point equivalence, $T$ would also prove the negation of that statement. Consistency therefore implies

$$
T\nvdash G.
$$

For this standard construction, the external argument also shows that $G$ is true in the standard natural numbers: there really is no standard finite $T$-proof of it.

$$
\mathbb N\models G,\qquad T\nvdash G.
$$

This conclusion uses the stated assumptions and the chosen standard proof predicate. It distinguishes standard arithmetic truth from provability in a fixed theory.

## Could the theory prove its negation?

Unprovability of $G$ alone does not show that neither side is provable. Gödel's original argument used **omega-consistency** to rule out a proof of $\neg G$.

A theory is omega-inconsistent if, for some formula $P$, it proves $\exists n\,P(n)$ while also proving $\neg P(\bar n)$ for every standard numeral $\bar n$.

If $G$ has no proof, each particular candidate number can be checked and rejected:

$$
T\vdash
\neg\operatorname{Proof}_T(\bar n,\ulcorner G\urcorner)
$$

for every standard $n$. Yet a proof of $\neg G$ would let $T$ assert

$$
T\vdash
\exists p\,\operatorname{Proof}_T(p,\ulcorner G\urcorner).
$$

Omega-consistency excludes this combination.

### Why ordinary consistency leaves room

A first-order theory of arithmetic can have **nonstandard models**. Such a model contains elements beyond every standard numeral. It can satisfy the assertion that some element has a property while every standard numeral fails it.

An element that the model regards as coding a proof may therefore be nonstandard. From outside, it is not the code of an ordinary finite derivation. Ordinary consistency alone does not make the second half of Gödel's original argument immediate.

In 1936, Barkley Rosser changed the self-referential construction. His refinement gives the familiar modern statement:

> **First incompleteness theorem.** Every consistent, computably axiomatized classical theory extending sufficient elementary arithmetic is incomplete. Some sentence $\varphi$ satisfies
>
> $$
> T\nvdash\varphi,\qquad T\nvdash\neg\varphi.
> $$

The arithmetic strength and effectiveness conditions matter. The collection of all true sentences of standard arithmetic is complete and consistent, but it has no computable enumeration of axioms.

## Adding an axiom

For an independent sentence $\varphi$, adding either $\varphi$ or its negation yields a consistent extension of $T$. We might choose one side on mathematical or philosophical grounds.

If the extension remains effectively axiomatized and sufficiently strong, incompleteness applies again. A new independent sentence appears.

For the particular Gödel sentence $G$, adding $G$ requires that $T$ does not already prove $\neg G$. This follows under the stronger assumptions used in Gödel's original two-sided argument. Rosser's construction avoids needing those assumptions for the existence of an independent sentence.

A missing decision can be supplied by a new axiom. The theorem rules out the hope that an effective, consistent theory of sufficient strength will thereby become a final account of all arithmetic truth.

## Expressing consistency inside arithmetic

The absence of a contradiction proof can also be arithmetized:

$$
\operatorname{Con}_T
\equiv
\neg\exists p\,
\operatorname{Proof}_T(p,\ulcorner0=1\urcorner).
$$

This is now a sentence in the language of arithmetic, constructed using the standard proof predicate for $T$.

Can $T$ prove that sentence?

### The second incompleteness theorem

For the usual provability predicate, a suitable theory can formalize the argument relating consistency to its Gödel sentence:

$$
T\vdash\operatorname{Con}_T\longrightarrow G.
$$

If it also proved $\operatorname{Con}_T$, it would prove $G$, contradicting the earlier argument when $T$ is consistent.

> **Second incompleteness theorem.** A consistent, computably axiomatized extension of PA cannot prove its own standard arithmetized consistency statement:
>
> $$
> T\nvdash\operatorname{Con}_T.
> $$

More general formulations specify the required arithmetic strength and derivability conditions carefully. The reference to the standard consistency statement matters: changing the meaning of “proof” is not a harmless way to bypass the theorem.

## Consistency after Gödel

A stronger theory can still prove the consistency of a weaker one. Relative consistency arguments remain available.

The pressure on Hilbert's program is more specific. If the proposed finitary justification can be formalized within the arithmetic it is supposed to secure, it cannot establish that arithmetic's consistency in the intended way. A method beyond that arithmetic may succeed, while raising a further question about why we accept the stronger method.

### Gentzen's example

In 1936, Gerhard Gentzen gave a consistency proof for PA using a suitable principle of transfinite induction on ordinal notations below $\varepsilon_0$.

Very roughly, a proof reduction lowers an assigned ordinal measure. The additional induction principle rules out the relevant endless descent, allowing a purported contradiction proof to be eliminated.

PA cannot justify the full induction principle needed for this argument. Gentzen's result therefore respects the second incompleteness theorem.

Proof theory continued by studying the strength of such extra principles. The question became: how much additional reasoning is needed to establish the consistency of a particular theory?

## What the boundary says

Cantor's diagonal construction escaped a proposed list. Russell's condition exposed the danger of unrestricted set formation. Gödel encoded syntax and used substitution to make a sentence concern its own provability.

Their results differ. A shared pattern is a construction that turns an organizing method back upon its own descriptions.

Incompleteness does not establish that humans always surpass machines, that formalization is futile, or that consistency can never be proved. Its statements concern specified formal theories under specified hypotheses.

One question remains from Hilbert's wider project. We have repeatedly used the words *mechanical*, *effective*, and *finite procedure*. To prove a limit on every possible algorithm, we need a mathematical account of computation itself.

That is Turing's starting point.
